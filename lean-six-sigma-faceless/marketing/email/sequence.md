# Email Sequence

Six emails, triggered by the Waste Walk opt-in. Tone matches the site: an operator
talking to another operator. Plain text, no header images, no "Hi {{first_name}}!"
theater.

**Sending rules**
- Double opt-in. ⚠️ Required in several jurisdictions and better for deliverability.
- One-click unsubscribe in every email.
- ⚠️ Physical address in the footer is a legal requirement in several jurisdictions
  (e.g. CAN-SPAM). **Confirm before sending a single email.**
- If someone replies, a human answers. That is the point of the reply CTAs.

**Timing:** #1 immediate · #2 day 2 · #3 day 5 · #4 day 9 · #5 day 14 · #6 day 45 (only
if no engagement).

## Email 2 — Value *(day 2)*

**Subject:** The bit that surprises people
**Preview:** It's almost never the work itself.

Did you get a chance to run it? If not, it keeps.

When people do run it, the same thing surprises them nearly every time. It is not that
any step takes too long. Every step is roughly fine.

It is the **waiting**.

The work takes an hour. The process takes three days. And the difference is almost
entirely sitting in an inbox, waiting on a decision, waiting on a callback, waiting
for someone to be free.

That matters because of where people usually look. When a job takes too long, the
instinct is to look at the person doing it — work faster, be more organized, try
harder. But if 90% of the elapsed time is waiting, then making the working part 20%
faster changes almost nothing. You optimized the hour and ignored the three days.

The second surprise is usually the K column — the steps only one person can do. Every
one of those is a step that stops when they are on vacation. Most owners know they have
one or two. Very few have counted.

If you have run the walk, look at your W and K rows again. That is usually where the
first real fix is hiding.

— The Lean Desk

*P.S. Stuck picking a process? Reply with what your week looked like and I'll tell you
which one I would start with.*

## Email 3 — Worked example *(day 5)*

**Subject:** Where the quote went wrong
**Preview:** A made-up example, with real arithmetic.

Let me walk one through, so it is concrete.

⚠️ *This is an illustrative example, not a client. When I have real ones I can show
you, they will have names and numbers on them.*

An eleven-person plumbing firm. Quotes go out wrong about twice a week — wrong
materials, wrong access assumptions, wrong hours. Each one costs roughly 40 minutes to
redo, and occasionally a job.

The instinct is "we need to be more careful when quoting."

The walk shows something different. The person quoting is working from a phone call
taken by whoever answered, written on whatever was to hand. Sometimes the caller
mentioned it was a third-floor apartment with no elevator. Sometimes they did not, because
nobody asked.

So the quote is not going wrong at the quote. It is going wrong at the phone call, two
steps upstream, and the person quoting is being asked to be careful with information
they were never given.

**The fix is not "be more careful."** It is five required questions on the call, in a
form that cannot be submitted half-empty.

The arithmetic: 2 × 40 minutes = 80 minutes a week, about 69 hours a year, plus the
occasional lost job.

Two honest caveats. That 69 hours is for *ranking* problems, not a forecast of cash —
some of it just becomes slack, and that is fine. And the fix only works if the person
answering the phone helped design the five questions. Hand them a form they did not
help write and it will be half-filled within two weeks.

That whole pattern — the problem is upstream of where it shows up — is most of what
this method is.

— The Lean Desk

## Email 4 — Objection *(day 9)*

**Subject:** "I don't have time for this"
**Preview:** The honest answer, including where it doesn't apply.

The most common reply I get is some version of: *this is sensible, and I do not have
time.*

I am not going to tell you that you do. You know your week better than I do.

But it is worth separating two things, because they get bundled together.

**"I don't have time to fix things"** is usually true and usually permanent, if the
fixing is a project. A program, a workshop, a consultant, six months. You are right
to refuse that. Most small businesses cannot absorb it, and the research on why lean
fails in small firms says exactly that — the frameworks were designed for large
companies with slack that you do not have.

**"I don't have two hours a week for four weeks"** is a different claim, and it is
worth checking, because it is roughly what fixing one real process costs.

Here is the test I would actually apply. Not "can I find two hours" — you cannot, there
are none spare. It is: **is there anything currently eating two hours a week that this
would stop?** If your worst problem is costing you three hours a week, then four weeks
at two hours a week pays for itself inside a month and keeps paying.

If it is costing you twenty minutes a week, leave it alone. Genuinely. Not everything
worth improving is worth improving now, and I would rather you kept your money than
bought something that ranks eleventh on your list.

That is what Step 4 of the Waste Walk is for — putting rough numbers on it so you can
tell the difference between the loudest problem and the most expensive one. They are
very often not the same problem.

— The Lean Desk

- ⚠️ Do not add urgency, discounts or scarcity to email 5. At $49 there is nothing to
  discount, and fake scarcity on an evergreen digital product is a lie the audience
  will spot.

  that label. Replace the whole email with a real case study once Test 6 delivers one.

  conversations in Test 4 — treat every one as a research interview.
